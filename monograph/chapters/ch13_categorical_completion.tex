\section{Introduction: The Three Foundational Problems}
\label{sec:cc_intro}
% ============================================================

\begin{multicols}{2}

The hard problem of consciousness, articulated by Chalmers~\citep{chalmers1995facing},
asks why there is subjective experience at all.  Systems can process information,
respond to stimuli, report internal states---yet seemingly lack phenomenal
consciousness, the ``what it is like'' of experience.  No third-person functional
description appears sufficient to account for first-person subjectivity.

Three classical responses have each failed:

\textbf{Eliminativism}~\citep{dennett1991consciousness}: consciousness is an
illusion.  Fails to account for why the illusion exists; contradicts direct
experience.

\textbf{Dualism}~\citep{chalmers1996conscious}: consciousness is non-physical.
Violates physical closure; provides no interaction mechanism.

\textbf{Panpsychism}~\citep{tononi2016integrated}: consciousness is a fundamental
property like mass.  Faces the combination problem; provides no mechanism for
why specific physical configurations produce specific experiences.

Our approach identifies consciousness as a \emph{derived physical process}
arising from categorical completion dynamics in oxygen-metabolizing systems.
The hard problem dissolves because consciousness is not an additional property
requiring explanation; it \emph{is} the process of filling oscillatory holes
created by oxygen movement in the \Hplus\ electric-field substrate.

This chapter is organized along three converging lines of evidence.  First,
olfactory receptor biophysics provides the canonical experimental test case:
vibrational rather than shape recognition demonstrates that BMDs implement
oscillatory circuit completion.  Second, the categorical completion framework
establishes that consciousness is identical to the hole-filling process.
Third, computational pharmacodynamics provides five-scale quantitative
validation: drugs are oscillatory-hole fillers (N-type biological carriers),
and their therapeutic action is information-catalytic resonance through
biological P-N junctions.

\begin{theorem}[Shape Theory Incompleteness]
\label{thm:shape_incompleteness}
Molecular shape alone cannot account for olfactory discrimination.  The

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{(1) Enantiomers}~\citep{amoore1964stereochemical}: mirror-image

(C--H $\to$ C--D) preserve all spatial and electronic properties except

different from protonated musk~\citep{franco2011deuterium}.
Shape theory predicts identical perception.  Observed: distinct perceptions.  (\emph{Falsifies shape theory.})

\textbf{(3) Musk diversity}: muscone ($C_{16}H_{30}O$), musk xylene
($C_{12}H_{15}N_3O_6$), galaxolide ($C_{18}H_{26}O$), and musk ambrette
($C_{12}H_{14}O_3$) are chemically and structurally unrelated yet all smell
like musk.  Shape theory has no principled explanation.  Vibrational theory:

\label{thm:iets}

\begin{equation}
eV = \hbar\omega_k
\label{eq:iets_resonance}
\end{equation}

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

$T_{\text{elastic}} \propto e^{-2\kappa d}$,
$\kappa = \sqrt{2m\Phi}/\hbar$.

$T_{\text{inelastic}} \propto e^{-2\kappa' d'}\cdot g_{e\text{-vib}}$,

\end{proof}
